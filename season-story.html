Our season story

From September 24 to November 20 we wrote 114 notebook entries. These are the twelve decisions that shaped our robot the most, in order. Each one links to the entries behind it.

Start: a plywood robot with two shooters
Now: CR1 v6, a Pollen robot with a topspin shooter on a turret

The short version

We started with a robot that would collect both balls and shoot each with its own shooter. We built and tested parts of it, and learned a lot about squeezing a ball and sorting by size. In November we made two big choices: focus on Pollen for the qualifier, and switch to a topspin shooter after our shots kept bouncing out of the hive. That new robot is CR1. We cleaned it up into CR1 v6, and a sponsor credit turned its main plates into aluminum. Next we build it and test it before the qualifier on December 20.

1Sep 24–25

Make the robot ourselves

We saw
Our laser cutter bed (305 × 381 mm) and printer beds (256 mm) are smaller than a robot side plate.
We chose
Build in-house from 1/4 in plywood and PLA anyway: split big plates with seams that step around holes, and use one bolt size (#8-32) for every wood joint.
Why
We can change a part the same day, and every student learns CAD, laser cutting and printing.

2Oct 4–7

Find the real cause before fixing the shooter

We saw
Our first pollen shooter barely shot: about 1–3 m/s, or the ball got stuck.
We chose
Go through the video frame by frame first. The wheel wasn't touching the ball. We made a rigid hood with a 63.5 mm gap (about 10% squeeze), instead of a heavier flywheel or faster gears.
Why
A heavier or faster wheel can't help if it never touches the ball.

3Oct 18–21

Sort the two balls by size, and test it on the bench

We saw
Pollen (71 mm) and Nectar (91 mm) come in through the same intake.
We chose
A 78 mm slot that only Pollen fits through. We built a bench kit before the robot: Pollen went into the slot 5 out of 5 times, Nectar got past it 6 out of 6.
Why
A cheap bench test found a problem (Nectar stuck on a lip) that a grip strip fixed in one afternoon.

4Oct 23–26

Copy what works

We saw
The strengthened pollen shooter v4 was built, bench tested and "worked great". The old nectar shooter design had the same no-squeeze problem.
We chose
Start the nectar shooter fresh as a copy of pollen v4, sized for the bigger ball.
Why
Starting from a design that already works was faster than patching the old one.

5Nov 1

Pollen only for the qualifier

We saw
We had no reliable way to drive Nectar into its shooter, and the drivetrain felt rough.
We chose
A Pollen-only robot for the first qualifier, with stronger end-tied drive pods. Nectar waits for later.
Why
One thing done well beats two things half done. It also freed space, weight and a motor.

6Nov 2–3

A short, full-width wedge for the flower

We saw
In video of another team, a wedge slid under the Pollen in the flower so it rolled out. Our first linkage reached 703 mm when deployed.
We chose
One full-width wedge on two short arms (at most 587 mm out in CAD).
Why
Fewer moving parts and it stays inside the size limit when deployed.

7Nov 4–11

Switch to a topspin shooter: CR1

We saw
In class footage, the shots that reached the hive bounced back out, 3 of 3 that we could follow. Our hood shooter gives the ball backspin.
We chose
A new robot, CR1, with the shooter wheel on top of the ball and a grippy ramp under it. We kept the drivetrain, odometry, hubs and wedge, and added a turret.
Why
Topspin makes the ball roll forward into the hive after it lands. Another team found the same when prototyping.

8Nov 13–16

Test rigs before the robot

We saw
CR1 had four new parts we had never built: the topspin head, the turret, the lane and transfer, and a new wedge pivot.
We chose
A small bench rig for each one, made from the real robot parts. The topspin head shot much more consistently on its rig, so we made it one printed piece with the motor under the wheel (9 parts to 1).
Why
A rig is cheap to change. Mistakes found there don't cost a whole robot.

9Nov 16–17

CR1 v6: fewer parts and a quarter inch to spare

We saw
CR1 had many one-off brackets and signs made of 16 parts, and only 0.35 mm of width to spare under 18 in.
We chose
A six-step clean-up: one bracket shape, one-piece signs, one chain idler, side plates cut whole, and the drive pods moved 5 mm in for 0.25 in on each side.
Why
A slightly proud bolt head could fail inspection. Fewer parts are faster to build and fix.

10Nov 18

Keep the wedge arms outside

We saw
Wedge arms outside the pods could be hit by other robots.
We chose
We fully modelled arms inside the pods, then kept them outside.
Why
Inside meant moving the wedge gears, a new arm channel and many changed parts. The outside design was already checked, and the qualifier was a month away. Not every idea we model gets picked.

11Nov 19

Metal where it matters

We saw
A sponsor gave us a $500 credit for metal parts, but the whole robot in metal would cost more.
We chose
The 7 plates that carry the wheels, motors and hits (side plates, motor plates, rear panel) in 1/8 in aluminum, with every part staying in the same place.
Why
Aluminum doesn't crush under nuts or split at bearing holes. Keeping every part in place meant no new clashes to chase.

12Nov 19–20

Keep every ball moving, and let the camera see

We saw
About 200 mm of the lane had nothing pushing a ball. The parked wedge blocked the camera at most turret angles.
We chose
A lane printed in 2 pieces (was 7 parts) with 3 small extra wheels, the camera on top of the shooter head, and 3 low wedge shapes to test on the real flower.
Why
A ball that stops in the lane is a lost shot. A camera that can always see the target helps the turret aim.

What's next

CR1 v6 is fully designed and checked in CAD, but not built yet. Before the qualifier on Sunday, December 20 we will:

  1. Cut the aluminum and plywood plates and print the parts.
  2. Build the robot and measure it against the 18 in box.
  3. Test with counts: pickups out of 20, shots in out of 10, flower pickups out of 20.
  4. Tune the shooter speed control and the turret aim, and write the results in the notebook.
